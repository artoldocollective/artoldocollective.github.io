Hitlr and the Teutonic Order

(fragment of a lecture, 1938 – reconstruction)

There are moments when an entire civilization seems to work toward the destruction of ancient sacral concentrations. Hierarchies are dissolved, personal dependencies reduced, symbolic mediations destroyed through the continuous extension of abstract relations. But this movement of dissolution, once it reaches a certain degree, itself produces a contrary demand. Absolute homogeneity becomes unliveable. A society entirely given over to the circulation of equivalences tends toward a kind of inner insufficiency. Everything within it becomes functional, yet this very functionality opens a region of vacancy where sacral forces begin once more to grow. No longer at the margins. At the center. Medieval Christianity, the chivalric order, the dynastic State, the armed nation: these realities must not be artificially isolated from one another. They represent different states of one and the same necessity for collective condensation. And it is precisely at this point that contemporary formations tend to rediscover, under entirely new conditions, something belonging to the oldest sacral communities: the possibility for human beings to participate in a totality that demands more than their adherence — that demands their consummation.

Modern Europe had dissociated the ancient forms of sovereignty. But a society entirely surrendered to equivalence necessarily produces an opposite desire. This desire does not merely move toward authority. It moves toward a lost density. Here we must recognize a fundamental ambiguity. For the movement through which a community attempts to recover a lost density may also become the movement through which it demands the increasing suppression of every internal heterogeneity. The greater the cohesion, the more intolerable difference becomes. Thus communities of this kind continually oscillate between two poles: the intensification of participation and the violent reduction of whatever escapes their form. Every homogeneous society disperses. It disperses beings into labor, into fragmentary utility, into the continuity without intensity of everyday exchanges. But certain formations seek instead to wrench individuals away from this dispersion. They concentrate them. They fix them around an inaccessible center that gradually acquires the value of a sovereign principle. Every society tends to produce a zone of maximum cohesion. But this cohesion, when it becomes absolute, transforms itself into pressure. And this pressure calls for an outlet.

National Socialism appears here as an attempt to give form to this outlet by mobilizing ancient figures of sacral sovereignty. The Teutonic Order is not invoked as nostalgia. It is invoked as a structural principle: radical separation of the elect body, intensification of obedience, and substitution of organic continuity by ritual continuity. But such a substitution can never remain stable. For the community thus constituted does not rest upon equilibrium, but upon extreme tension. It demands constant expenditure, a permanent putting into play of bodies and existences. It therefore necessarily tends toward a point at which it can no longer preserve itself as form. Modern society — we know this — tends toward increasing homogeneity. It dissolves the ancient forms of separation; it reduces intensities to usable equivalences. It had reduced human relations to calculable relations. The State itself tended to become an administration of equivalences. Everything within human existence that belongs to unproductive expenditure, prestige, violence without immediate economic purpose, is rejected outside the regular field of social life. But this rejection is never definitive. It produces returns. The Hitlrian phenomenon must be approached from this angle. Not as a simple historical regression, but as the constitution of a form of community whose cohesion rests precisely upon the

violent reintroduction of the heterogeneous into the social body. The Teutonic Order must not be considered one historical precedent among others. Such a way of seeing still belongs to the profane intelligence of history, which arranges dead forms within an inert succession. But what is at stake here is not succession. It is persistence. Certain forms traverse history in a latent state. They may remain deprived of visible effectiveness for long periods. Yet they never cease exerting an obscure pressure upon the very structures of collective life. National Socialism did not restore the ancient sacral forms. It rendered their return compatible with the technical organization of the modern world. But the present phenomenon introduces a further transformation. The ancient order still remained limited by the organic slowness of historical structures. Its power of diffusion remained relatively restricted. Modern technique entirely modifies this condition. Administrative and technical modernity has made possible the resurrection of a much older form of community. It permits the amplified reactivation of archaic forms of sacral community. This is not a matter of nostalgia for ancient forms. It would be too simple — and already false — to believe that Europe seeks to restore what it destroyed. Forms never return

as they disappeared. They return transformed, displaced, intensified. What is recomposing itself before our eyes today is not a reactivated past, but a new coincidence between two orders that until now seemed incompatible: the technical order and the sacral order. The one presupposes the neutralization of intensities. The other presupposes their extreme concentration. And yet they coexist. They coexist within one and the same historical structure where the instruments of administrative modernity, far from dissolving archaic powers, instead offer them an unprecedented effectiveness. The Teutonic Order represents one of the most accomplished historical forms of this concentration. National Socialism does not represent the simple return of ancient imperial or chivalric structures. It represents something more troubling: the technical possibility of their reactivation. The modern apparatus no longer destroys ancient sacral forms. It amplifies them. It is within these returns that what we call, for lack of a better term, the sacred is constituted. We find ourselves confronted with a historical form that resists ordinary analysis. Not because it is obscure, but because it exceeds the categories through which we attempt to dispose of it. What is at stake here is not a doctrine, nor even a political

system in the sense we ordinarily understand. It is an organization of collective forces, a mode of crystallization of social energy. And herein lies its most troubling character: not in its doctrine, but in its internal logic. In this sense, certain modern political formations can no longer be understood as ideologies in the classical sense. They are dispositifs of affective condensation. It must therefore be understood that the modern power of certain political organizations does not derive essentially from their doctrine. Doctrines change relatively quickly. Forms of affective participation change much more slowly. A community may abandon its explicit beliefs while preserving intact the emotional structure that made them possible. It is in this sense that sacral forms survive their declared contents. Not systems of thought. But machines of linkage. This displacement becomes visible today in the way certain political formations organize adhesion. What is involved is not rational adherence. It is a total affective participation in which the individual is absorbed into a collective figure that exceeds every utility. People have often wanted to reduce this phenomenon to propaganda. But propaganda explains nothing if it is not itself understood as the secondary effect of a deeper necessity: that of producing a community capable of sustaining an intensity superior to ordinary life.

Thus we rediscover a structure Europe believed it had surpassed: that of the armed sacred order, distinct from the common world and yet charged with its maximum intensity. Modern societies, in abolishing orders of this type, did not suppress their necessity. They merely displaced their return. It is impossible to understand this form without recourse to ancient structures of European history. The Teutonic Order in particular appears here not as an antiquarian survival, but as a matrix. A matrix of separation, discipline, and election. A form in which war, far from being a simple political function, becomes a condition of spiritual existence. This point must be insisted upon: in such formations, war is not a means. It is a form of life. It is what gives consistency to the community itself. It is here that the Teutonic Order takes on its operative value. It is not a historical reference. It is a form. A form that returns. A form that imposes separation, obedience, and above all an economy of sacrifice. Now every economy of sacrifice tends to exceed its own limits. Here we touch upon a region where politics ceases to be intelligible as the administration of human affairs and becomes a limit-experience of community itself. For what is sacrificed is never merely offered: it is consumed by the community itself. And this consumption engages the community in a dynamic it cannot stabilize.

Thus there emerges a logic that we cannot reduce to politics: a logic of intensification. The question is therefore not: how is such a movement possible despite modernity? The question is: what does modernity itself produce once it reaches a certain degree of internal tension? The answer cannot be formulated in terms of progress or decadence. It must be formulated in terms of cohesion and rupture. Here immediate moral judgments must be suspended. Not because they are illegitimate, but because they prevent us from seeing the phenomenon in its own structure. We are not confronted with an error of history, but with a possibility internal to history itself. Order does not merely represent a political necessity. We must already break with this impoverished interpretation. Order does not primarily serve to govern. It serves to separate. Order does not merely serve to maintain a stable organization of social functions. It introduces among beings a distance, a quality of distance, that immediately modifies their manner of existing. From this moment onward, certain men cease to belong to common circulation. They no longer live according to the ordinary measure of labor and exchange. They form a distinct body.

But a distinct body cannot maintain itself without discipline. And discipline itself calls for an ordeal. This separation must therefore be tested in risk. Not the individual risk of adventure, but a shared, continuous risk capable of giving the group its maximum affective density. Only at this point does the community tend toward the combative form. Every strong community begins with a separation. Not juridical separation. Not that of classes. Another kind. A separation that touches bodies themselves. There then exist beings who no longer participate in the ordinary circulation of things. Their existence ceases to be measured by common utility. They enter a region where life becomes immediately tied to risk, discipline, and a constant possibility of death. It is insufficient to say that such military formations produce a superior cohesion. What they produce first is an affective condensation. An intensification of presence. Every gesture ceases to be isolated. Every gesture engages the totality. The Teutonic Order may be nothing other, in European history, than the first great durable attempt to constitute a community in which individual existence disappears within a continuity more violent than simple political continuity. And it is precisely this violent continuity that reappears today.

It is not enough to say that it united war and religion. Such a formula remains external. What matters lies elsewhere: in the constitution of a separated body whose every member progressively ceased to belong to himself. Order absorbs particular existences. It does not merely annul them. It tears them away from the ordinary continuity of human exchanges in order to introduce them into a continuity that is denser, more rigorous, and at the same time more dangerous. From this moment onward, community ceases to be the simple grouping of individuals. It becomes a reality superior to those who compose it. But such a reality can only maintain itself through a constant ordeal. This is why formations of this type always tend toward discipline, closed hierarchy, and the permanent possibility of sacrifice. Sacrifice does not come afterward. It is already present in the very structure of separation. We must here return to what we mean by community. Modern community, such as bourgeois society produced it, rests upon the substitution of circulation for intensity. Beings are linked there by equivalences, not by participations. But this substitution does not destroy the need for participation. It renders it more violent. More unstable. It is within this instability that the forms we are examining here emerge.

The Teutonic Order, in this perspective, is not a historical model. It is a persistent figure of absolute separation. A community that defines itself not by inclusion, but by ordeal. A community whose existence presupposes continuous expenditure, internal tension, and a constant relation to the risk of dissolution. When such forms reappear within modernity, they never do so in a pure state. They graft themselves onto the very instruments that make them possible: administration, technique, rational organization of the masses. It is this grafting that produces the most disconcerting effect. For what we call modernity is not destroyed by these forms. It becomes the condition of their effectiveness. The administrative mobilization of the masses now allows sacral forms to penetrate the entire social body with a rapidity unknown to ancient societies. It is here that the present situation acquires its true gravity. For technique no longer dissolves the ancient powers of fascination. It extends them. It propagates them. It gives them a capacity for hold that earlier societies could never have conceived. Thus there is constituted a historical zone in which the distinction between archaic and modern ceases to be operative.

Not because it has been theoretically surpassed, but because it has become insufficient to describe the reality of the forces at play. The question is no longer whether a society is modern or premodern. The question is to recognize the forms of intensity it makes possible. What then appears within certain contemporary formations is not merely the resurgence of ancient symbols of power. It is the reappearance, at the very heart of the technical world, of a truth older than political doctrines themselves: that no human community can attain an extreme degree of intensity without simultaneously calling forth the powers of destruction from which that intensity proceeds. And it is precisely here that the previously posed question returns with increased insistence: Can there exist a sacral community that is not closed within national fixation — that is, one that does not stabilize itself as a closed identity, a fixed symbolic territory, an assigned historical destiny? Or is every attempt to reactivate the sacred within modern history immediately captured by a form of assignment, closure, and destiny? This question is not theoretical. It is the very question of our age. It thus becomes impossible naïvely to oppose modernity and archaism.

The archaic no longer subsists outside the modern world as a residual survival. It now circulates within the most advanced apparatuses of rational organization themselves. Modern administration therefore ceases to be neutral. It becomes the vehicle of intensities that exceed it. But a community carried to this degree of intensity cannot remain immobile. Fixity is impossible for it. Such a formation preserves itself only on the condition of ceaselessly increasing the tension that constitutes it. From that point onward, discipline no longer suffices. The ordeal must become continuous. Risk must penetrate existence in its entirety. What is then revealed is no longer merely a structure of authority, but a dynamic of consummation. For the sacral community does not simply seek to endure. It tends obscurely toward a point where its own intensity becomes incompatible with any durable stability. The denser collective participation becomes, the more expenditure it requires. And expenditure itself calls forth ever greater forms of mobilization. Thus there appears an almost mechanical necessity of expansion, conflict, violent surpassing of prior limits. War then ceases to be a political accident. It becomes the moment in which the community attains for itself the feeling of its deepest reality.

For such a form does not stop once it reaches its maximum cohesion. It is precisely at that moment that it begins to undo itself otherwise — not through weakening, but through excess. The sacral community can never durably coincide with the stable order of productive societies. It demands something else. It demands that the beings engaged within it progressively cease to be reducible to the simple conservation of themselves. This is why formations of this type always maintain an ambiguous relation to death. It would be insufficient to say that they accept death. Many societies accept it. What distinguishes them is something else: they tend to make exposure to death a condition of participation itself. From this moment onward, community is no longer founded merely upon shared interest, nor even upon common belief, but upon a shared possibility of consummation. Order then appears in a new light. It no longer represents merely an organization of functions or a hierarchy of powers. It becomes an operation exercised against the ordinary dispersion of human existences. This is why such formations tend toward a progressive simplification of the human world.

Not an intellectual simplification, but an affective simplification. Everything must become immediately legible according to the opposition between pure and impure, faithful and foreign, integrated body and separating element. But this simplification never constitutes a state of equilibrium. It instead produces an increasing agitation. For the more a community seeks to attain absolute unity, the more it encounters internally whatever resists this unification. It then becomes impossible clearly to distinguish what belongs to discipline from what already belongs to a form of collective vertigo. Obedience itself changes nature. It ceases to be merely political. It becomes participation in a reality experienced as superior to individual life. At this point, community practically ceases to be a human association in the ordinary sense. It becomes a destiny. But a collective destiny thus constituted can no longer accept the ordinary limits of historical existence. It necessarily tends toward ever greater forms of putting-intoplay. As though the community could verify its own reality only by exposing itself to the risk of destruction. And perhaps it is here that modern sacral forms once again rejoin the oldest forms: in this obscure necessity of expending themselves beyond every conservative measure.
